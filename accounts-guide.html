TFSA vs RRSP vs FHSA

The account you choose can matter as much as the investment itself. Here’s how Canada’s main registered accounts work, how the tax math changes, and where each dollar should go first.

The short answer

Take the employer match. Use an FHSA for a first home. Then choose TFSA or RRSP based on income and flexibility.

Know the containers

Five accounts. Five different jobs.

These are account types, not investments. You still choose what to hold inside—cash, GICs, ETFs, stocks, bonds or funds.

Flexible wealth

TFSA

$7,000 2026 annual limit
$109,000 cumulative room since 2009 if fully eligible

  • After-tax money goes in; growth and withdrawals are tax-free.
  • Withdraw any time, for any reason.
  • There is no forced withdrawal and no lifetime contribution cap beyond accumulated room.
Withdrawal room returns January 1 of the next calendar year—not immediately. An early recontribution can trigger a 1% monthly overcontribution tax.
Retirement + tax deduction

RRSP

18% of 2025 earned income
$33,810 2026 maximum

  • Contributions are deductible; withdrawals are fully taxable income.
  • $10,000 contributed at a 29.65% marginal rate creates about a $2,965 tax reduction.
  • Employer match is an immediate return. Take all of it before moving down the waterfall.
  • The Home Buyers’ Plan can allow up to $60,000 for a first home, repaid over 15 years.
  • Convert to a RRIF by December 31 of the year you turn 71.
First-home advantage

FHSA

$8,000 annual limit
$40,000 lifetime limit

  • Tax-deductible contribution, tax-free growth and tax-free qualifying first-home withdrawal.
  • An $8,000 contribution at 29.65% can reduce tax by about $2,372.
  • Room starts only after you open the account—even if the balance is $0.
  • If you never buy, transfer the balance to an RRSP tax-free without using RRSP room.
  • First-time buyer generally means no home ownership in the current year or four prior calendar years.
Education + government grant

RESP

$50,000 lifetime per child
$500 annual basic CESG on $2,500 contributed

  • Contributions are not tax-deductible.
  • The basic CESG adds 20% on the first $2,500 contributed each year.
  • Basic CESG has a $7,200 lifetime maximum per child.
  • Grant and growth are taxed to the student when withdrawn for education.
Unlimited, but unsheltered

Non-registered account

No contribution limit

  • Interest and dividends can be taxable each year; realized capital gains use a 50% inclusion rate in this example.
  • Best used after valuable registered room has been filled.
  • If you do use one, tax-efficient holdings and low turnover can help reduce yearly tax drag.
Decision framework

Fund the highest-value opportunity first.

This general-purpose waterfall balances free employer money, homeownership benefits, tax rates and flexibility.

01

Employer match

Contribute enough to capture the full RRSP or pension match. A 50% match is an instant 50% return before investing.

02

FHSA

Buying a first home within the account’s time window? Aim for the $8,000 annual room.

03

TFSA or RRSP

Choose based on your current tax bracket, expected retirement bracket and need for flexible withdrawals.

04

RESP

With children, target $2,500 per year per child to capture the basic $500 CESG—without sacrificing your own retirement.

05

Non-registered

Use after registered room is full. Be deliberate about tax efficiency.

Lower income or starting out

TFSA often comes first: the current deduction may be smaller, while withdrawals stay flexible and don’t count as taxable income.

Higher income

RRSP often becomes more valuable: deduct contributions at a higher rate, then ideally withdraw at a lower rate in retirement. Investing the refund in your TFSA strengthens the strategy.
